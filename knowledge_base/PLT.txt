Indicator: PLT (Platelet Count)
Full name: Platelet Count (Thrombocyte Count)

Reference range: 100 – 300 × 10⁹/L
Unit: × 10⁹/L (equivalent to 10³/µL or K/µL)

Clinical significance:
Platelets are tiny cell fragments that initiate clot formation at the site of vessel injury to stop bleeding. The platelet count determines the body's ability to form a primary haemostatic plug.

HIGH (thrombocytosis, > 300 × 10⁹/L) — common causes:
- Iron-deficiency anaemia (reactive thrombocytosis)
- Acute or chronic infection and inflammation
- Post-splenectomy (the spleen normally removes old platelets)
- Essential thrombocythaemia (a myeloproliferative disorder — rule out when PLT > 600 × 10⁹/L)
- Post-surgical or post-traumatic reactive increase

LOW (thrombocytopenia, < 100 × 10⁹/L) — common causes:
- Immune thrombocytopenic purpura (ITP — autoimmune destruction)
- Drug-induced (heparin — HIT, chemotherapy, certain antibiotics)
- Viral infections (dengue fever, EBV, HIV)
- Sepsis (severe bacterial infection)
- Bone marrow failure or suppression
- Disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC)
- Liver cirrhosis (hypersplenism increases platelet destruction)

Critical thresholds:
- < 50 × 10⁹/L  : Risk of spontaneous bruising, nosebleeds, prolonged bleeding from minor cuts
- < 20 × 10⁹/L  : Risk of serious spontaneous bleeding (GI, intracranial) — urgent evaluation needed

Notes:
- Mildly low PLT (80–100 × 10⁹/L) without bleeding symptoms may simply be monitored.
- A very high PLT (> 1 000 × 10⁹/L) can paradoxically increase thrombosis risk.
- Combine with coagulation studies (PT, APTT) for a complete haemostatic assessment.
