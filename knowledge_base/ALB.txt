Indicator: ALB (Albumin)
Full name: Albumin

Reference range: 35 – 50 g/L
Unit: g/L

Clinical significance:
Albumin is the most abundant protein in human blood and is synthesised exclusively by the liver. It has three key roles:
1. Maintains oncotic pressure (keeps water inside blood vessels and prevents oedema)
2. Transports hormones, fatty acids, drugs, bilirubin, and other substances
3. Reflects the liver's synthetic capacity and overall nutritional status

Because albumin has a half-life of ~20 days, it reflects the liver's synthetic function over a period of weeks — making it a marker of chronic rather than acute liver damage.

HIGH (rare):
- Dehydration (relative concentration — haemoconcentration)

LOW (hypoalbuminaemia, ALB < 35 g/L) — common causes:
- Liver cirrhosis or severe liver dysfunction (most important cause — reduced synthesis)
- Malnutrition (inadequate protein intake, common in the elderly, chronic illness)
- Nephrotic syndrome (massive urinary albumin loss)
- Chronic inflammatory disease (acute-phase response diverts synthetic resources)
- Extensive burns (protein lost through skin)
- Malabsorption syndromes (Crohn's disease, coeliac disease)
- Advanced malignancy (increased catabolism)

Severity grading:
- 30–35 g/L : Mild — usually well tolerated
- 25–30 g/L : Moderate — peripheral oedema may develop
- < 25 g/L  : Severe — risk of ascites, significant peripheral and pulmonary oedema

Notes:
- Albumin < 28 g/L is commonly associated with visible lower-limb oedema and ascites.
- Albumin is one of five variables in the Child–Pugh score used to assess cirrhosis severity.
- Both treating the underlying cause and optimising nutritional support are necessary to raise albumin levels.
