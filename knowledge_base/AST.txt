Indicator: AST (Aspartate Aminotransferase)
Full name: Aspartate Aminotransferase (formerly SGOT — Serum Glutamic-Oxaloacetic Transaminase)

Reference range:
- Males:   10 – 40 U/L
- Females: 10 – 35 U/L
Unit: U/L

Clinical significance:
AST is present in the liver, heart muscle, skeletal muscle, kidneys, and red blood cells. Because it is less liver-specific than ALT, it is best interpreted in combination with ALT and other markers rather than in isolation.

HIGH — common causes:
- Liver diseases (hepatitis, fatty liver, cirrhosis, hepatocellular carcinoma)
- Acute myocardial infarction (begins to rise 6–12 hours after onset — now largely replaced by troponin)
- Skeletal muscle injury (intense exercise, rhabdomyolysis, muscle trauma)
- Alcoholic liver disease (AST/ALT ratio > 2 is a hallmark of alcoholic hepatitis)
- Drug-induced liver injury
- Haemolysis (RBCs contain AST — haemolysed specimens give falsely high values)

LOW: no clinical significance.

AST/ALT ratio — diagnostic value:
- Ratio < 1   : Suggests viral hepatitis or NAFLD (ALT rise predominates)
- Ratio 1–2   : Non-specific; may be seen in cirrhosis or chronic hepatitis
- Ratio > 2   : Strongly suggests alcoholic liver disease
- Ratio > 3   : Highly specific for alcoholic hepatitis

Notes:
- An isolated AST elevation without elevated ALT should prompt cardiac enzyme testing (CK, troponin) to rule out a cardiac event.
- After intense exercise, both AST and CK may be transiently elevated — blood should be drawn after adequate rest.
- AST and ALT should always be read together; the ratio provides more diagnostic information than either value alone.
