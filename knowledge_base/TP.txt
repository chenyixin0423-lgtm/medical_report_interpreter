Indicator: TP (Total Protein)
Full name: Total Protein

Reference range: 65 – 85 g/L
Unit: g/L

Clinical significance:
Total protein = albumin (ALB) + globulins (GLB).
Globulin is calculated as: GLB = TP − ALB  (normal: ~20–30 g/L)
The albumin-to-globulin ratio (A/G) reflects the balance between the liver's synthetic function and the immune system's activity.

A/G ratio:
- Normal: 1.2 – 2.4
- Decreased A/G (< 1.0): chronic liver disease, cirrhosis, multiple myeloma
- Inverted A/G (< 1.0): signals serious hepatic synthetic impairment

HIGH (hyperproteinaemia) — common causes:
- Dehydration (haemoconcentration)
- Multiple myeloma or Waldenström's macroglobulinaemia (marked globulin overproduction — TP can exceed 90–100 g/L)
- Chronic infections or chronic inflammation (polyclonal globulin increase)
- Autoimmune diseases (SLE, chronic active hepatitis)

LOW (hypoproteinaemia) — common causes:
- Malnutrition (inadequate dietary protein)
- Severe liver dysfunction (reduced albumin synthesis)
- Nephrotic syndrome (urinary protein loss)
- Malabsorption syndromes
- Large-volume IV fluid administration (haemodilution)
- Advanced cancer (increased catabolism)

Notes:
- A low TP is most clinically meaningful when evaluated alongside ALB; a very low ALB with a normal or high globulin points to specific conditions (e.g. cirrhosis with ongoing inflammation).
- TP > 90 g/L with a normal ALB suggests a major globulin excess — serum protein electrophoresis (SPEP) is indicated to exclude a plasma cell dyscrasia.
- The A/G ratio and serum protein electrophoresis together provide a clearer picture of the nature of any protein abnormality.
