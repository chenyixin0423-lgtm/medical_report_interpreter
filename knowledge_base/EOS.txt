Indicator: EOS (Eosinophils)
Full name: Eosinophil Count / Eosinophil Percentage

Reference range:
- Percentage (EOS%): 0.5 % – 5 %
- Absolute count (EOS#): 0.02 – 0.52 × 10⁹/L
Unit: % or × 10⁹/L

Clinical significance:
Eosinophils are specialised white blood cells that defend against parasitic worms and regulate allergic and inflammatory reactions. An elevated count is a classic marker of allergic disease or parasitic infection.

HIGH (eosinophilia) — common causes:
- Allergic conditions (asthma, allergic rhinitis, eczema, urticaria) — most common
- Parasitic infections (roundworms, hookworms, schistosomiasis, Toxocara)
- Drug hypersensitivity reactions
- Eosinophilic oesophagitis or gastroenteritis
- Autoimmune diseases (coeliac disease, inflammatory bowel disease)
- Rare: hypereosinophilic syndrome or eosinophilic leukaemia (EOS# > 1.5 × 10⁹/L, persistent)

LOW (eosinopenia):
- Usually has no direct clinical significance
- Seen with corticosteroid therapy (steroids suppress eosinophils)
- Acute bacterial infection or severe physiological stress (early phase)

Severity grading (absolute count):
- Mild:     0.5 – 1.5 × 10⁹/L  — often allergic or drug-related
- Moderate: 1.5 – 5.0 × 10⁹/L  — parasitic infection or drug reaction likely
- Severe:   > 5.0 × 10⁹/L      — hypereosinophilic syndrome must be excluded; risk of organ damage

Notes:
- Recent travel to tropical regions with a raised EOS warrants stool microscopy and serology for parasites.
- Eosinophilia combined with skin rash, fever, and lymphadenopathy may indicate drug reaction with eosinophilia and systemic symptoms (DRESS) — seek urgent evaluation.
