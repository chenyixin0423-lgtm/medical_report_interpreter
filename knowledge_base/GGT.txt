Indicator: GGT (Gamma-Glutamyl Transferase)
Full name: Gamma-Glutamyl Transferase (γ-GT)

Reference range:
- Males:   10 – 60 U/L (some laboratories use 50 U/L)
- Females: 7 – 45 U/L
Unit: U/L

Clinical significance:
GGT is found mainly in the liver (bile duct epithelium), kidney, and pancreas. It is highly sensitive to liver disease and is particularly useful as a marker of biliary obstruction and alcohol consumption.

HIGH — common causes:
- Alcoholic liver disease (GGT is extremely sensitive to alcohol — even moderate regular drinking elevates it; it falls significantly with abstinence and is a useful sobriety marker)
- Biliary obstruction / cholestasis (gallstones, cholangitis, bile duct cancer)
- Non-alcoholic fatty liver disease
- Viral hepatitis
- Drug-induced liver injury (especially enzyme-inducing drugs: phenobarbitone, phenytoin, rifampicin; some traditional herbal remedies)
- Pancreatic disease
- Hyperthyroidism (elevated thyroid hormones induce GGT)

LOW: no clinical significance.

GGT + ALP — key combination:
- GGT ↑ + ALP ↑  → strongly suggests biliary/cholestatic disease
- GGT ↑, ALP normal → likely alcohol or drug effect on the liver
- ALP ↑, GGT normal → likely bone origin (ALP is also produced by bone)

Notes:
- GGT is the most alcohol-sensitive routine liver enzyme; a rise after claimed abstinence can indicate ongoing drinking.
- Certain medications (enzyme inducers) can double or triple GGT as a normal pharmacological effect — not a sign of liver damage.
- Isolated mild GGT elevation with normal ALT, AST, and ALP often resolves with alcohol abstinence or stopping the offending drug; recheck in 4–8 weeks.
