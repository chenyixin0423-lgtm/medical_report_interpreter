Indicator: DBIL / IBIL (Direct Bilirubin / Indirect Bilirubin)
Full name: Direct (Conjugated) Bilirubin / Indirect (Unconjugated) Bilirubin

Reference range:
- DBIL (direct bilirubin):   0 – 6.8 µmol/L
- IBIL (indirect bilirubin): 1.7 – 10.2 µmol/L  (= TBIL − DBIL)
Unit: µmol/L

Clinical significance:
Understanding the difference between the two fractions of bilirubin helps pinpoint where in the bilirubin pathway the problem lies:

- Indirect bilirubin (IBIL): the water-insoluble form produced from haem breakdown in the spleen — not yet processed by the liver. High levels can cross the blood-brain barrier and are toxic.
- Direct bilirubin (DBIL): the water-soluble, liver-conjugated form that is excreted into bile. Elevated levels indicate impaired biliary excretion.

Classifying jaundice type by DBIL/TBIL ratio:

IBIL elevated, DBIL/TBIL < 20 % → Haemolytic (pre-hepatic) jaundice:
- Haemolytic anaemia, transfusion reactions
- Neonatal physiological jaundice
- Gilbert's syndrome or Crigler–Najjar syndrome (genetic enzyme deficiency)

DBIL elevated, DBIL/TBIL > 50 % → Obstructive (post-hepatic) jaundice:
- Common bile duct stones
- Stricture, bile duct cancer, or pancreatic head tumour compressing the duct
- Drug-induced cholestasis

Both elevated, DBIL/TBIL 20–50 % → Hepatocellular (hepatic) jaundice:
- Acute or chronic viral hepatitis
- Liver cirrhosis or hepatocellular carcinoma
- Alcoholic hepatitis

Notes:
- DBIL and IBIL are components of TBIL; they must be read together with the total value.
- An isolated mild IBIL elevation with a normal DBIL is most commonly Gilbert's syndrome — benign, no treatment needed.
- Dark urine (bilirubinuria — a tea-coloured appearance) correlates with elevated DBIL and can be an early sign of bile duct obstruction or hepatocellular disease.
